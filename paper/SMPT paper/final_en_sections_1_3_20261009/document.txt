1. Introduction
    Epidemics spread at more than one scale. Inside a community, infection may pass through a detailed
network of contacts between people (Keeling and Eames, 2005; Pastor-Satorras et al., 2015; Kiss et al.,
2017). Between communities, the available data are often much less detailed. For example, they may give
only passenger numbers, commuting ows, or a weighted link between two places (Balcan et al., 2009;
Barbosa et al., 2018). We therefore need a model that can use a detailed network inside each community
and simpler data between communities.
    This situation is common in practice. A local contact network may be observed, reconstructed, or
generated to match known properties such as clustering and mean shortest-path length (Eubank et al.,
2004; Danon et al., 2011). In contrast, links between communities may be known only through transport,
migration, commuting, or mobile-phone data (Barbosa et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2021). Such data can tell
us the total strength of connection between two communities, but not which people form the connection.
We may therefore know the total coupling without knowing the bridge nodes or their infection states.
    If every contact is known, a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) can describe the epidemic, and a
Gillespie-type method can simulate it exactly (Gillespie, 1977; Gibson and Bruck, 2000). Ecient methods
are also available for temporal, large, and sparse networks (Vestergaard and Génois, 2015; Kuryliak et al.,
2025). However, these methods still need the individual contacts that may transmit infection. Metapopulation
models use aggregate ows between communities, but usually also replace the detailed local network by
population counts (Colizza et al., 2007; Colizza and Vespignani, 2008; Balcan et al., 2010; Ajelli et al.,
2010). Our problem lies between these two cases: we want to keep node-level stochastic spread inside every
community while using only aggregate information between communities.
    The idea of a two-scale simulator was presented in an earlier conference paper (Kuryliak and Emmerich,
2025). It runs a separate local simulator for each community. The macro layer uses the current community
states and connection weights to generate infections arriving from another community. In this article, we
describe the method in detail and study its approximation error. In particular, we examine what is lost

   Our main question is: when is this aggregate connection sucient to reproduce the quantity of interest in

the total weights of the cross-community connections.
   This article makes four contributions.

  1. We describe how the simulator works: each community is simulated separately, and a shared scheduler
      determines infections between them. We explain how it coordinates the local simulators on time
      intervals of xed length (Section 3, Algorithm 1).
  2. We show which network the method simulates exactly. It is exact for a surrogate network : the real

      weight (Proposition 1). We then identify the sources of error and analyse a single export of infection
     (Proposition 2).
  3. We compare the two-scale method with full-network Gillespie simulation for transmission to one
      neighbouring community and for spread along chains of communities. We measure dierences using the
      relative shift in the median time of rst infection and the Wasserstein distance between the distributions

2. Related work
Simulation of stochastic events. Gillespie's direct method draws the next event and its time from the
current event rates (Gillespie, 1977). The next reaction method keeps a separate clock for each possible
event (Gibson and Bruck, 2000). Both methods are exact for the CTMC they simulate. When an event rate
changes with time, its rate can be accumulated until it reaches a random threshold (Ethier and Kurtz, 1986).
Anderson (2007) used this idea for systems with time-dependent rates and delays. Our macro clock uses the
same idea, but its rate changes because the local epidemic states change. Network simulators can save work
by updating only the rates aected by the latest event (Vestergaard and Génois, 2015; Kuryliak et al., 2025).
Our local simulators use the implementation of Kuryliak et al. (2025).
   Some multiscale methods speed up chemical-reaction models by approximating reactions that occur much
faster than others (Haseltine and Rawlings, 2002; Cao et al., 2005). Our method removes a dierent kind
of detail. It keeps the local infection events, but does not know which individual cross-community edges are
currently able to transmit. Even so, the relative speeds of local spread and cross-community transmission
help explain its error.


Models with several communities. Metapopulation models represent each subpopulation by counts and
connect the subpopulations by mobility ows. They have been used to study global spread and the conditions
for invasion (Colizza et al., 2007; Colizza and Vespignani, 2008; Balcan et al., 2009, 2010). Detailed mobility
data can also be combined with local transmission models (Chang et al., 2021). Agent-based models instead
represent individuals throughout the model (Eubank et al., 2004; Kerr et al., 2021). Ajelli et al. (2010)
compared these two broad approaches. The method studied here keeps individual nodes and contacts inside
each community, but replaces the unknown individual contacts between communities by one aggregate
stochastic interface.

Two levels of mixing and co-simulation. Some epidemic models combine contacts in small local groups
with uniform mixing at a wider level (Ball et al., 1997). Our surrogate network is related to this idea,
but uniform mixing is used only between connected pairs of communities, and its strength is set by their
aggregate weight.
   The implementation also has features of co-simulation. In co-simulation, separate subsystem solvers move
forward on a shared time grid and exchange a limited amount of information (Gomes et al., 2018). Here, the
local community simulators are those subsystems. They send their infected and susceptible counts to the
macro layer. In this implementation, those exchanged counts are used to draw a cross-community infection
time and then to choose its receiving community and target node. The scheduler can therefore treat the
local simulators as black boxes.



3. The two-scale simulation method
   The experiments use one implementation of the two-scale simulator, in which the local simulators
exchange information with the macro scheduler at xed time intervals. To understand its error, we also
consider a process with the same rates but exact timing of every event. We call this theoretical description
the ideal process. It separates the error caused by changing the network from the additional error caused by
the time grid. The ideal process is exact for the surrogate network dened later; this does not make it exact
for the original physical network. Below, we explain the reference model, the available data, and the steps
of the implemented simulator.

3.3. Local simulators
   Each community has a separate Gillespie simulator for its local graph Gi . It applies the infection and
removal rates in (2) using only the local edges Eii . The implementation updates only rates aected by
the latest event, following Kuryliak et al. (2025). Each local simulator is independent and has its own
random-number stream.
   The macro layer can advance a local simulator to a specied time. The simulator applies the local events
up to that time. The macro layer can then import an infection by changing one selected susceptible node to
I . This changes the local rates. The implementation keeps the unused part of the random threshold for the
next local event and recalculates its waiting time using the new total rate.

its own network. The approximation comes from the way imports are generated and their target nodes are
selected.

   The macro layer represents each community by its current fractions and represents each directed
connection by Wij . Its rate for an infection from community i to community j is

The factors have a direct meaning: βout Wij gives the total connection strength, ȳi is the infected fraction
in the source, and s̄j is the susceptible fraction in the receiving community. The sum Θ is the rate of any
cross-community infection. Calculating the waiting time to the next event when Θ varies with time requires
special treatment (Section 3.5).
    When such an event occurs, rst select the source and receiving communities (i, j) with probability
λij /Θ. Then choose one susceptible node uniformly in the receiving community j and infect it. If there
are 20 susceptible nodes, each has probability 1/20. This selection does not use real bridge nodes,
because the aggregate interface does not know them. In later formulas, we write this target probability
as Πij (v | x) = 1/sj for v ∈ Sj , where x is the current network state. Thus, Π simply denotes the probability
of choosing a particular susceptible node. Other target rules can be used if bridge-node information is
available.
   This aggregate rate is generally an approximation to transmission through the real physical edges. For
xed community counts, it is the expected rate under uniformly random selection of the edge endpoints,
before their particular identities, states, and epidemic history are taken into account. This does not guarantee
the correct rate for an epidemic already realised on specic edges. For uniform all-to-all coupling, it is the
actual rate, as explained in Section 4.
