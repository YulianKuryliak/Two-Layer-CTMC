1. Introduction
    Epidemics spread at more than one scale. Inside a community, infection may pass through a detailed
network of contacts between people (Keeling and Eames, 2005; Pastor-Satorras et al., 2015; Kiss et al.,
2017). Between communities, the available data are often much less detailed. For example, they may give
only passenger numbers, commuting ows, or a weighted link between two places (Balcan et al., 2009;
Barbosa et al., 2018). We therefore need a model that can use a detailed network inside each community
and simpler data between communities.
    This situation is common in practice. A local contact network may be observed, reconstructed, or
generated to match known properties such as clustering and mean shortest-path length (Eubank et al.,
2004; Danon et al., 2011). In contrast, links between communities may be known only through transport,
migration, commuting, or mobile-phone data (Barbosa et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2021). Such data can tell
us the total strength of connection between two communities, but not which people form the connection.
We may therefore know the total coupling without knowing the bridge nodes or their infection states.
    If every contact is known, a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) can describe the epidemic, and the
Gillespie method can simulate it exactly (Gillespie, 1977; Gibson and Bruck, 2000). Ecient methods are
also available for temporal, large, and sparse networks (Vestergaard and Génois, 2015; Kuryliak et al., 2025).
However, these methods still need the individual contacts that may transmit infection. Metapopulation
models use aggregate ows between communities, but usually also replace the detailed local network by
counts of people in dierent states (Colizza et al., 2007; Colizza and Vespignani, 2008; Balcan et al., 2010;
Ajelli et al., 2010). Our problem lies between these two cases: we want to keep node-level stochastic spread
inside every community while using only aggregate information between communities.
    The idea of a two-scale simulator was presented in an earlier conference paper (Kuryliak and Emmerich,
2025). It runs a separate local simulator for each community. The macro layer uses the current community
states and connection weights to generate infections arriving from another community. In this article, we
describe the method in detail and study its approximation error. In particular, we examine what is lost
when real cross-community edges and their endpoints are replaced by the total connection weight, and an
imported infection is placed on a randomly selected susceptible node.
    Our main question is: when is this aggregate connection sucient to reproduce the quantity of interest in
the full-network CTMC? We study infection arrival times, their distributions, and whether transmission is
possible through the real contacts. Similar arrival times do not guarantee a possible transmission route: the

2. Related work
Simulation of stochastic events. Gillespie's direct method draws the next event and its time from the
current event rates (Gillespie, 1977). The next reaction method keeps a separate clock for each possible
event (Gibson and Bruck, 2000). Both methods are exact for the CTMC they simulate. When an event rate
changes with time, its rate can be accumulated until it reaches a random threshold (Ethier and Kurtz, 1986).
Anderson (2007) used this idea for systems with time-dependent rates and delays. Our macro clock uses the
same idea, but its rate changes because the local epidemic states change. Network simulators can save work
by updating only the rates aected by the latest event (Vestergaard and Génois, 2015; Kuryliak et al., 2025).
Our local simulators use the implementation of Kuryliak et al. (2025).
    Some multiscale methods speed up chemical-reaction models by approximating reactions that occur much
faster than others (Haseltine and Rawlings, 2002; Cao et al., 2005). Our method removes a dierent kind
of detail. It keeps the local infection events, but does not know which individual cross-community edges are
currently able to transmit. Even so, the relative speeds of local spread and cross-community transmission
help explain its error.

Models with several communities. Metapopulation models represent each subpopulation by counts of
people in dierent states and connect the subpopulations by mobility ows. They have been used to study
global spread and the conditions for invasion (Colizza et al., 2007; Colizza and Vespignani, 2008; Balcan
et al., 2009, 2010). Detailed mobility data can also be combined with local transmission models (Chang
et al., 2021). Agent-based models instead represent individuals throughout the model (Eubank et al., 2004;
Kerr et al., 2021). Ajelli et al. (2010) compared these two broad approaches. The method studied here keeps
individual nodes and contacts inside each community, but replaces the unknown individual contacts between
communities by one aggregate stochastic interface.

Two levels of mixing and co-simulation. Some epidemic models combine contacts in small local groups
with uniform mixing at a wider level (Ball et al., 1997). Our surrogate network with uniform inter-community

    The implementation also has features of co-simulation. In co-simulation, separate subsystem solvers move
forward on a shared time grid and exchange a limited amount of information (Gomes et al., 2018). Here,
the local community simulators are those subsystems. They send their infected and susceptible counts to
the macro layer. The macro layer uses these data to generate the time of a cross-community infection and
select the receiving community and target node. The local simulators can therefore remain black boxes for
the scheduler. In this implementation, the exchanged data are used to create random infection events.


3. The two-scale simulation method
   The experiments use one implementation of the two-scale simulator, in which the local simulators
exchange information with the macro scheduler at xed time intervals. To understand its error, we also
consider a process with the same rates but exact timing of every event. We call this theoretical description
the ideal process. It separates the error caused by changing the network from the additional error caused by
the time grid. The ideal process is exact for the surrogate network with uniform inter-community connections
dened later; this does not make it exact for the original physical network. Below, we explain the reference
model, the available data, and the steps of the implemented simulator.

3.6. Computational structure and intended use
   Full stores all local and cross-community channels, and its work per event grows with node degree
(Kuryliak et al., 2025). TwoScale stores the local graphs and an M × M matrix of aggregate weights. The
conference paper discusses this reduction in the information stored for cross-community contacts (Kuryliak
and Emmerich, 2025). Its importance depends on the problem. Replacing many individual cross-community
edges by one weight can reduce storage. If only a few such edges exist, this saving may be small, and the
local graphs still have to be stored.
   The separate local simulators also provide an opportunity for parallel execution. Once the scheduler
has chosen the next target time (tg or t∗ ), the local simulators can advance independently to that same
time. The midpoint copies can also be processed in parallel. The macro layer must then collect the counts
and apply any scheduled import before continuing. The implementation studied here advances the local
simulators sequentially; parallel execution within one run is a possible extension, not a measured speedup.
Local simulators can also use dierent community-specic settings.

neither lower memory use nor faster execution is guaranteed. The method is useful when individual cross-
community contacts are unavailable or too numerous to represent explicitly.

4. Theoretical analysis of the two-scale method
    The previous section described the two-scale simulation algorithm. The remaining question is how closely
its results represent infection spread in the original network. Although the local simulators reproduce the
within-community processes exactly, the macro layer uses aggregate connections instead of individual inter-
community edges.
    In this section, we rst identify the network that mathematically corresponds to the ideal two-scale
process. This establishes what changes when contacts are aggregated and separates these changes from the
numerical synchronisation error. We then investigate how losing information about bridge nodes aects the
rst transmission time and introduce a characteristic for analysing accuracy in the experiments.

4.1. Equivalence to simulation on a network with uniform inter-community
     connections
    The two-scale method replaces explicit inter-community contacts with aggregate infection rates while
retaining exact stochastic dynamics within communities. This raises the question of whether the resulting
process can be represented as exact simulation on a contact network and, if so, what that network looks like.
    To answer this question, consider the ideal two-scale process, in which the time of each macro event is
determined exactly by (5), without the time-grid approximation. This process is a continuous-time Markov
chain (CTMC), because its transition rates depend on the current system state. Local transitions have the
same rates as in the full-network model, while inter-community infections are determined by the aggregate
rates (4) and the recipient-node selection rule.
    We show that, when the susceptible recipient node is selected uniformly, this process is equivalent to
exact simulation on a surrogate network with uniform inter-community connections, G̃ (Figure 1c). In this
network, the total weight of each inter-community connection is distributed equally among all node pairs in
the corresponding communities.

4.2. Mechanisms of discrepancy from the full-network model
   By construction, the surrogate network G̃ preserves within-community contacts and the total coupling
rate between each community pair:
         X
                β̃uv = βout Wij .

    This equality concerns the total rate of all channels, however, rather than only those through which
infection can be transmitted in the current system state. The actual infection rate depends on the endpoint
states of each contact. Equal total inter-community weights therefore do not guarantee the same infection
dynamics.
    Replacing real inter-community contacts by averaged connections changes three things: the transmission
rate, the recipient node, and the possible number of imports. A fourth source of error is the approximate
calculation of event times in the implementation.

1. The states of the real bridge nodes are not used. In the full network, the transmission rate from Vi
to Vj is

      a⋆ij (x) = βout               wuv 1{xu = I, xv = S},                                                   (7)
                        (u,v)∈Eij

Only contacts from infected nodes to susceptible nodes contribute to this sum. If all bridge nodes in the
source are still susceptible, transmission is impossible. In the two-scale method, the rate depends on the
overall infected fraction, so infection of any source node can contribute to export. The method can transmit
infection before any real bridge node is infected. We refer to this as bridge bypass (Section 4.3).
    The aggregate rate does not necessarily exceed the actual rate. If infected nodes are concentrated at
the inter-community interface, the opposite can occur. Comparing rates in an individual state is therefore
insucient to establish the overall direction of a shift in transmission time.

2. Import location. In the full network, infection reaches the endpoint of the contact through which it was
transmitted. The two-scale method selects any susceptible node with equal probability. As a result, infection
may reach a node that has no incoming contact from the corresponding source community in the original
network. The location of the rst infection aects subsequent spread within the community, including the
time taken to reach bridge nodes leading to further communities. Changing the import-placement rule can
therefore aect both local dynamics and infection spread through a sequence of communities.

3. Additional inter-community infections. In the SI model, each node can move from S to I only once.
Each directed channel in the original network can therefore cause at most one infection of its target node.
Consequently, at most |Eij | such infections can occur through the bundle Eij .
   The two-scale method does not track individual inter-community channels. After the rst import, the
rate λij can remain positive as long as the source community has infected nodes and the recipient community
has susceptible nodes.

4. Numerical synchronisation error. As described in Section 3.5, the scheduler estimates the rate from
the system state at the midpoint of a synchronisation interval and uses it to approximate the integral (5).
The implemented TwoScale can therefore dier even from the ideal two-scale process on G̃.

Remark 1 (The rst import with randomly placed contacts). Suppose that the endpoints of inter-
community channels are selected uniformly and independently of the internal graphs and other contact sets,
as in our tests. If the recipient community is still fully susceptible, the target of its rst import is also
uniform in the full network, after averaging over random contact placement. The two-scale method has the
same distribution of the rst target. For an individual xed network, the targets need not coincide. The same
distribution of the rst target does not imply the same distribution of the rst import time. Our tests show
small errors in a number of congurations where the spread of internal infection times is small compared
with the mean transmission time through one inter-community channel (Section 6.4).

   Replacing inter-community contacts thus changes not only the nodes through which infection can
be transmitted, but also the possible locations and number of infections. These mechanisms can act
simultaneously and should not be treated as independent additive components of the total error. Analysing
them alone does not establish either the overall direction or the magnitude of discrepancies between the
models.

5.1. Experimental networks and parameters
   The theoretical analysis in the previous section showed how aggregating inter-community contacts
changes infection transmission and under which conditions a stochastic shift in the rst export time arises.

However, it does not determine the magnitude of the discrepancies between the models or their behaviour
after successive inter-community crossings.
    To investigate these questions, we compare Full and TwoScale in four experimental series. We rst

Cliqueleaf. To compare the rst export times of the two methods with minimal inuence from internal

Random communityleaf. We next investigate how the internal structure of the source aects infection
transmission to the leaf. We replace the clique with a connected random graph of n = 1000 nodes and attach
the leaf through k ∈ {1, 5} distinct bridge nodes.

    The initially infected node is selected uniformly from all source nodes. For each conguration, we perform
400 runs of each model: 40 realisations of the internal graph with 10 replicates of inter-community contacts
and random events. We use β = 0.003 and a horizon of 30 000.

Chain of cliques. The preceding tests consider just one inter-community crossing. To examine how
discrepancies change after successive crossings, we connect L = 10 complete graphs, each with n = 1000
nodes, in a line, with one edge between neighbouring communities (k = 1). Infection starts in community 0.
    As in the rst test, complete graphs provide rapid internal spread. This allows us to assess the eect of
successive inter-community crossings in a baseline setting with minimal internal path lengths.
    We use β = 0.003, 400 runs of each model, and a horizon of 30 000.

Chains of random communities. Finally, we combine successive inter-community crossings with variation
in internal community structure. This series examines how community size and internal structure aect
discrepancies between Full and TwoScale, and how these discrepancies change along the chain.
    We connect L = 10 connected random communities in a line, with k ∈ {1, 5} edges between neighbouring
communities. Infection starts in community 0.
    We consider sizes n ∈ {50, 100, 250, 1000}. Internal graphs are generated by the procedure described
above, varying ASPL from 1 to 6 in steps of 1.
    For each interface, we select k nodes uniformly without replacement from each community and connect
them in pairs. A node can participate in interfaces with both neighbouring communities.
    For each conguration, we perform 400 runs of each model with β = 0.003 and a horizon of 30 000. Two

5.3. A measure of the ratio of time scales
   Discrepancies between Full and TwoScale may depend not only on network structure, but also on
the relative speeds of infection spread within and between communities. In particular, aggregation does not
account for when individual bridge nodes become infected, which can matter when internal spread is slow
and uneven.
   To investigate this relationship, we use the dimensionless measure
     ρ = βout σT ,                                                                                        (9)
where σT is the standard deviation of node infection times within a community. This measure describes the
spread of internal infection times relative to the mean transmission time through one continuously active
inter-community channel, 1/βout .
    For each conguration, we calculate σT from node infection times in each source community of the Full
runs, then average these values over communities and runs.
    We examine how ρ relates to the median shift and normalised Wasserstein distance for the arrival time
in the last community, T9 . In particular, we compare how well ρ and ASPL explain discrepancies between
the models across community sizes and structures.
    A small ρ means that the spread of internal infection times is small relative to the transmission time
through one channel. We test whether such congurations show smaller discrepancies between the models.
    For each conguration, σT is calculated from node infection times in the source communities of the Full
runs and averaged over these communities and runs.
    In the results, we compare ρ with the median shift and normalised Wasserstein distance across community
sizes, internal structures, and numbers of inter-community edges.
    The measure ρ is not a direct error metric. We consider it a possible explanatory characteristic of the
discrepancies. Since σT is calculated from Full, we do not test whether error can be predicted from local
simulations alone.

5.4. Statistical evaluation
   Because the simulations are stochastic and the number of runs is limited, the estimated discrepancies
between Full and TwoScale have sampling uncertainty. We use a block bootstrap to assess this uncertainty
and a permutation test to test dierences between infection-time distributions.

                     Two-scale stochastic simulation with aggregate inter-community coupling


Block bootstrap. Replicates on the same graph share its structure and are therefore not independent
realisations of a network conguration. To account for this dependence, the bootstrap samples whole network
blocks with replacement, keeping all ten replicates and their corresponding pairs of Full and TwoScale
runs together.
    For chain summary statistics, we form 2000 bootstrap samples and recalculate the measures of interest.
The limits of the 95% condence intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting estimates.
This assesses uncertainty in the magnitude and direction of discrepancies between the models.

Permutation test. The Wasserstein distance can be positive even for two samples from the same
distribution, because each contains a nite number of random observations. We therefore need to test whether
the observed distance exceeds the level that can arise from sampling variability.
    For each pair of runs, we randomly swap the observed Full and TwoScale infection times with
probability 1/2. We recalculate the normalised Wasserstein distance from the resulting samples. Repeating
this procedure 2000 times gives a distribution of distances under random reassignment of results between
models.
    Under the null hypothesis that Full and TwoScale results are exchangeable within pairs, we use this
distribution to assess the statistical signicance of the observed distance. Its 95th percentile denes the
permutation threshold, and the fraction of permutations with a distance at least as large as the observed
one estimates the p-value.
    Exceeding the threshold indicates a statistically signicant discrepancy under the assumed null model.

5.5. Validation of the Full implementation
   To validate the full-network simulator, we compare the simulated mean infection time of the rst bridge
node, TB , with the analytical value for a clique (Appendix A). This check uses four clique sizes and two
numbers of inter-community edges.
   We also check the mean transmission time to the leaf after the single bridge becomes infected. For one
continuously active channel, its theoretical value is 1/β .
   These checks assess the agreement of the Full implementation with known analytical results, but do
not establish the accuracy of the two-scale approximation.

6.4. Relationship between discrepancies and the ratio of time scales
    The results suggest that discrepancies between the models may depend on how quickly infection spreads
within a community relative to inter-community transmission.
    To examine this relationship, we use the measure ρ = βout σT dened in Section 5.3. It describes the spread
of internal infection times relative to the mean transmission time through one inter-community channel.
    Figure 7(a,b) shows the normalised Wasserstein distance eW and median shift b9 as functions of ρ for all
48 chain congurations.
    The measure ρ had a stronger rank association with eW than target ASPL. The Spearman correlation
between ρ and eW was 0.91 across all congurations and 0.98 for congurations with ASPL ≥ 3. The
corresponding values for ASPL were 0.82 and 0.66.
