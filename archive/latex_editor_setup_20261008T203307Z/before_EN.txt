Abstract
Multiscale stochastic simulation often couples detailed subsystem dynamics
through aggregate interfaces. We study this problem for epidemic spreading on
community networks, where within-community contact structure is represented
explicitly while inter-community coupling is available only in aggregate form.
Each community is evolved by a Gillespie engine that is exact conditional
on the imported events it receives; the macro layer generates imports from
aggregate, state-dependent hazards and, in the default implementation, places
them uniformly among susceptible nodes of the receiving community. We give
a complete specification of the method, including its grid-based synchronisation,
and show that its ideal continuous-time version is an exact simulation of a
surrogate network in which each bundle of inter-community edges is replaced by
uniform all-to-all coupling of equal total weight. This representation identifies
four sources of discrepancy: loss of boundary-state information, uniform import
placement, absence of interface-edge exhaustion, and synchronisation error. For
uniformly random interfaces in the single-export setting, we further show that
the ideal aggregate export clock is stochastically earlier than the full-network
one. Controlled experiments on synthetic SI networks compare the method
with full-network Gillespie simulation in single-export benchmarks and in 48
configurations of ten-community chains (50–1000 nodes per community, internal
path lengths 1–6, one or five interface edges, 400 matched runs each). When the
spread of infection times within a community was below a quarter of the mean
single-edge crossing time, terminal arrival-time distributions were statistically
indistinguishable in 15 of 18 configurations; beyond this regime, the two-scale
simulator arrived early by an amount roughly proportional to that ratio, reaching
40%. Close timing did not imply admissible routes: up to 52% of aggregate
crossings preceded infection of any true bridge node.

1. Introduction
Epidemics spread at more than one scale. Inside a community, infection may pass through a detailed
network of contacts between people (Keeling and Eames, 2005; Pastor-Satorras et al., 2015; Kiss et al.,
2017). Between communities, the available data are often much less detailed. For example, they may give
only passenger numbers, commuting flows, or a weighted link between two places (Balcan et al., 2009;
Barbosa et al., 2018). We therefore need a model that can use a detailed network inside each community
and simpler data between communities.
This situation is common in practice. A local contact network may be observed, reconstructed, or
generated to match known properties such as clustering and mean shortest-path length (Eubank et al.,
2004; Danon et al., 2011). In contrast, links between communities may be known only through transport,
migration, commuting, or mobile-phone data (Barbosa et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2021). Such data can tell
us the total strength of connection between two communities, but not which people form the connection.
We may therefore know the total coupling without knowing the bridge nodes or their infection states.
If every contact is known, a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) can describe the epidemic, and a
Gillespie-type method can simulate it exactly (Gillespie, 1977; Gibson and Bruck, 2000). Efficient methods
are also available for temporal, large, and sparse networks (Vestergaard and Génois, 2015; Kuryliak et al.,
2025). However, these methods still need the individual contacts that may transmit infection. Metapopulation
models use aggregate flows between communities, but usually also replace the detailed local network by
population counts (Colizza et al., 2007; Colizza and Vespignani, 2008; Balcan et al., 2010; Ajelli et al.,
2010). Our problem lies between these two cases: we want to keep node-level stochastic spread inside every
community while using only aggregate information between communities.
The basic two-scale simulator was presented in an earlier workshop paper (Kuryliak and Emmerich,
2025). It runs one stochastic local simulator for each community. A separate macro layer uses the current
community states and the aggregate coupling to generate infections that enter from another community.
The present article gives a full description of this method and studies its approximation error. In particular,
we ask what is lost when real cross-community edges and their endpoints are replaced by an aggregate
connection, and what is lost when an imported infection is placed on a selected susceptible node.
Our main question is: when does this aggregate interface reproduce the behaviour of the full-network
CTMC well enough for the quantity of interest? We study arrival times, their distributions, and the physical
validity of the transmission route. In particular, similar arrival times do not prove that the route was
physically possible: the aggregate model can export infection before any real bridge node has been infected.
The complete microscopic network is known in our controlled experiments, so it can be used as a reference.
The two-scale simulator receives only the local networks and aggregate connections. These experiments test
the method; they are not a calibration of one particular epidemic.
This article makes four contributions.
1. We give a complete description of the two-scale simulator, including the time-grid scheduler used in
the experiments (Section 3, Algorithm 1).
2. We show exactly what the ideal continuous-time method simulates. It is exact for a surrogate network:
the real cross-community edges are replaced by weak all-to-all connections with the same total weight
(Proposition 1). It is not, in general, exact for the original physical network. We then identify the
resulting sources of error and analyse a single export (Proposition 2).
3. We compare the method with full-network Gillespie simulation for single exports and chains of
communities. Permutation baselines show how much difference can arise from finite sampling alone
(Sections 5 and 6).
4. We give an empirical indicator of accuracy for our benchmarks. It compares the spread of infection
times inside a community with the typical time needed to cross one interface edge (Section 6.6).
We consider susceptible–infected (SI) dynamics on static synthetic networks and use one fixed
synchronisation step. Section 2 explains the relation to earlier methods. Section 3 describes the simulator.
Section 4 explains what the aggregate interface preserves and loses. Sections 5 and 6 present the experiments
and results. Section 7 discusses use, cost, and limitations.

2. Related work
Simulation of stochastic events. Gillespie’s direct method draws the next event and its time from the
current event rates (Gillespie, 1977). The next reaction method keeps a separate clock for each possible
event (Gibson and Bruck, 2000). Both methods are exact for the CTMC they simulate. When an event rate
changes with time, its rate can be accumulated until it reaches a random threshold (Ethier and Kurtz, 1986).
Anderson (2007) used this idea for systems with time-dependent rates and delays. Our macro clock uses the
same idea, but its rate changes because the local epidemic states change. Network simulators can save work
by updating only the rates affected by the latest event (Vestergaard and Génois, 2015; Kuryliak et al., 2025).
Our local simulators use the implementation of Kuryliak et al. (2025).
Some multiscale methods speed up chemical-reaction models by approximating reactions that occur much
faster than others (Haseltine and Rawlings, 2002; Cao et al., 2005). Our method removes a different kind
of detail. It keeps the local infection events, but does not know which individual cross-community edges are
currently able to transmit. Even so, the relative speeds of local spread and cross-community transmission
help explain its error.
Models with several communities. Metapopulation models represent each subpopulation by counts and
connect the subpopulations by mobility flows. They have been used to study global spread and the conditions
for invasion (Colizza et al., 2007; Colizza and Vespignani, 2008; Balcan et al., 2009, 2010). Detailed mobility
data can also be combined with local transmission models (Chang et al., 2021). Agent-based models instead
represent individuals throughout the model (Eubank et al., 2004; Kerr et al., 2021). Ajelli et al. (2010)
compared these two broad approaches. The method studied here keeps individual nodes and contacts inside
each community, but replaces the unknown individual contacts between communities by one aggregate
stochastic interface.
Two levels of mixing and co-simulation. Some epidemic models combine contacts in small local groups
with uniform mixing at a wider level (Ball et al., 1997). Our surrogate network is related to this idea,
but uniform mixing is used only between connected pairs of communities, and its strength is set by their
aggregate weight.
The implementation also has features of co-simulation. In co-simulation, separate subsystem solvers move
forward on a shared time grid and exchange a limited amount of information (Gomes et al., 2018). Here, the
local community simulators are those subsystems. They send their infected and susceptible counts to the
macro layer. In this implementation, those exchanged counts are used to draw a cross-community infection
time and then to choose its receiving community and target node. The scheduler can therefore treat the
local simulators as black boxes.

3. The two-scale simulation method
The experiments use one implementation of the two-scale simulator, with a time grid for coordinating the
communities. To understand its error, we also consider the process that the same rates would define if every
event time were calculated exactly. We call this theoretical description the ideal process. It separates the
error caused by changing the network from the additional error caused by the time grid. The ideal process is
exact for the surrogate network defined later; this does not make it exact for the original physical network.
Below, we explain the reference model, the available data, and the steps of the implemented simulator.

3.3. Local simulators
Each community has a separate Gillespie simulator for its local graph Gi. It applies the infection and
removal rates in (2) using only the local edges Eii. The implementation updates only rates affected by the
latest event, following Kuryliak et al. (2025). Each local simulator has its own random-number stream.
The macro layer can ask a local simulator to move to a specified time. The simulator applies the local
events up to that time. The macro layer can then import an infection by changing one selected susceptible
node to I. This changes the local rates. The implementation keeps the unused part of the random threshold
for the next local event and recalculates its waiting time using the new total rate. It then selects the event
type and node using the updated rates. A new local threshold is drawn only if there is no existing one.
Each local clock has its own threshold, separate from the macro threshold described in Section 3.5. For
prescribed import times and target nodes, the local simulator follows the CTMC rules of its own network.
The approximation comes from the way the macro layer generates and places imports.

infection that changes a community count.

3.6. Computational structure and intended use
Full stores all local and cross-community channels, and its work per event grows with node degree
(Kuryliak et al., 2025). TwoScale stores the local graphs and an M × M matrix of aggregate weights. The
workshop paper discusses this reduction in the information stored for cross-community contacts (Kuryliak
and Emmerich, 2025). Its importance depends on the problem. Replacing many individual cross-community
edges by one weight can reduce storage. If only a few such edges exist, this saving may be small, and the
local graphs still have to be stored.
The separate local simulators also provide an opportunity for parallel execution. Once the scheduler
has chosen the next target time (tg or t∗
), the local simulators can advance independently to that same
time. The midpoint copies can also be processed in parallel. The macro layer must then collect the counts
and apply any scheduled import before continuing. The implementation studied here advances the local
simulators sequentially; parallel execution within one run is a possible extension, not a measured speedup.
Local simulators can also use different community-specific settings.
The grid scheduler adds work by copying local states and coordinating the communities. Therefore,
neither lower memory use nor faster execution is guaranteed. The method is useful when individual cross-
community contacts are unavailable or too numerous to represent explicitly. Section 7.2 reports the measured
run times; this article primarily evaluates approximation error.

5.3. Statistical procedures
Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a bootstrap that resamples whole network blocks,
keeping their ten replicates together, with 2000 resamples for chain summaries. Because eW is positive even
when two samples come from the same law, we calibrate it by a within-pair permutation test: the labels of the
two runs in each matched pair are swapped with probability 1/2, and eW is recomputed 2000 times. Under
the null hypothesis that matched runs are exchangeable, this yields a sampling floor, which we summarise
by its 95th percentile, and a p-value for the observed eW . Appendix B gives an analytical approximation of
the floor that applies when per-run data are not available.
The event seeds of a replicate depend on the target ASPL, the network block and the replicate index,
but not on n or k; seeds are therefore shared across community sizes and interface widths (common random
numbers). This reduces the variance of comparisons between such cells, but it also correlates their sampling
errors. We therefore do not count agreement between cells as independent replication.

7. Discussion
7.1. Practical guidance
The results suggest a simple procedure for deciding whether aggregate coupling is adequate.
Decide which observable matters. For the timing of spread between communities, the two-scale simulator
can be accurate. For transmission routes, it cannot be trusted. Up to half of its crossings in sparse
communities, and a fifth of exports even where export-time distributions agreed, occurred before any true
bridge node was infected. Questions that depend on bridge nodes, such as targeting interventions at the
individuals who connect communities or attributing an outbreak to a source, require boundary information
that the aggregate interface does not contain.
Estimate the time-scale ratio. Compute ρ = βoutσT . Since σT describes spread within a single community,
it can in principle be estimated from the local engines alone, by simulating an isolated community from a
uniformly placed seed. We computed it from the reference runs and have not tested this local estimate. In our
benchmarks, terminal arrivals were statistically indistinguishable for ρ . 0.25. For 0.3 . ρ . 3, TwoScale
arrived early by roughly 15ρ%. This rule is an empirical summary of one family of synthetic networks and
parameters, not a general error law.
Check absolute as well as relative errors. Along a chain, the relative discrepancy was nearly constant, so
the absolute lead at distant communities grew in proportion to the travel time. For intervention timing at
a specific destination, the absolute discrepancy at that destination is the relevant quantity.
Calibrate distances against sampling variability. With a few hundred runs per model, distances of a
few percent between arrival-time distributions are expected even between identical models (Appendix B).
Permutation baselines or analytical floors should accompany such comparisons.
7.2. Computational cost
The benchmarks were designed for accuracy assessment, so their explicit interfaces contain at most five
edges and the full reference is cheap. In this regime, aggregation removes almost no work, and the cost of grid
synchronisation dominates. The median wall-clock time per replicate of the Python implementations ranged
from 0.04–0.07 s (Full) and 0.6–31 s (TwoScale) for n = 50 to 8.6–18 s and 6.7–124 s for n = 1000. For
n = 1000, the ratio of the two medians ranged from 0.76 (ASPL 2, k = 5) to 6.9 (ASPL 6, k = 1).
Two conclusions follow. First, in settings where the full network is known and small, Full should be
preferred. Second, the overhead of the two-scale method stems from the black-box scheduler rather than from
aggregation. An event-driven scheduler that integrates the piecewise-constant hazard exactly (Section 3.5)
would remove both this overhead and the synchronisation error. Computational gains are expected when
inter-community contacts are numerous or unavailable, which is the setting the method is intended for
(Kuryliak and Emmerich, 2025); quantifying them is left to future work.
7.3. Limitations
The experiments use SI dynamics. With recovery, bridge nodes can stop transmitting while the
community-wide fraction remains positive, and the aggregate rate may then also overestimate exports late
in a local outbreak. All networks are synthetic, the macro-topology is a chain, and internal and external
per-edge rates are equal. Interfaces were placed at random, which by Remark 1 neutralises placement error for
first imports. Structured interfaces, for example with bridges at hubs or with incoming and outgoing bridges
far apart, may behave differently. The synchronisation step was not varied, so the numerical component of
the discrepancy was not isolated; Proposition 1 provides a direct way to do so. Finally, ρ was evaluated with
reference runs and chosen among a small number of candidate scalings; its predictive value for other network
families remains to be tested.
7.4. Possible extensions
The analysis points to modifications that use only aggregate information. Interface exhaustion can be
represented by limiting the number of imports per bundle to its number of channels, which is known whenever

8. Conclusions
The two-scale method couples exact local Gillespie engines through aggregate inter-community hazards.
In its ideal form, it is exact simulation on a surrogate network in which every interface is smeared uniformly
over all pairs of nodes. This identifies what the method loses: the state of the actual bridge nodes, the
location of imports and the finite capacity of thin interfaces. For randomly placed interfaces, it also implies
that aggregate export clocks are stochastically early. In controlled comparisons with full-network Gillespie
simulation, terminal arrival-time distributions were statistically indistinguishable when infection spread
through communities quickly relative to the time needed to cross an interface edge. As the internal timing
spread approached and exceeded that crossing time, the two-scale simulator arrived systematically early, by
up to 40% in small sparse communities. The ratio ρ = βσT organised these results across community sizes,
internal structures and interface widths. Close timing never guaranteed admissible transmission routes. The
method is therefore a reasonable tool for propagation-time questions when ρ is small. It is not suited to
questions about individual transmission routes, or to sparse communities with long internal paths unless the
interface is refined.

C. Reproducibility details
Seeds are derived deterministically from the experiment parameters. Each TwoScale engine draws
from a private pseudorandom stream; midpoint probes copy the stream state of their engine and do
not advance it. Two Full runs (n = 50, ASPL 6, k = 1) had not reached the tenth community by
T = 30 000. They were rerun to T = 40 000 with the same graphs, initial nodes and event seeds. Their
event histories up to 30 000 were verified to match the saved runs, and their terminal arrivals occurred
at about 31 059 and 31 007. No horizon value was substituted for an unobserved event. The permutation
and bootstrap analyses of the chain configurations, the descriptor σT and Figures 6–7 were computed
from the saved runs by the scripts extrascripts/analysis/smpt_twoscale_paper_metrics.py and
extrascripts/visualization/smpt_twoscale_paper_figures.py.
